## Define the measurement before choosing a model

Record the instrument or asset, unit being measured, ownership rights, measurement date, purpose and reporting framework. Determine why a fair-value measurement is being considered. A management forecast, transaction price, tax appraisal and financial-reporting estimate can answer different questions even when each is called “value.”

Keep the source of each input, its observation date, its currency and unit, and any changes made to it. A date on the report cover may be its preparation date, not the date of the underlying market observation. A precise decimal output cannot fix an unmatched instrument or missing fact.

## An original instrument-matching exercise

All records and values below are invented. Assume the workpaper concerns 1000 units of Instrument A at September 30. The packet contains three price observations. No source has been validated as an applicable fair-value input.

| Record | Quoted amount | Instrument and observation date | Open question |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Q-A | USD 98 per unit | Instrument A, September 30 | Executable price, market context, unit and restrictions still need verification |
| Q-B | USD 104 per unit | Instrument B, September 30 | Different described rights; no conversion into A is established |
| Q-C | USD 101 per unit | Instrument A, June 30 | Earlier observation; intervening facts remain unknown |

Multiplying 1000 by 98 gives USD 98000. Substituting Q-B gives USD 104000, a USD 6000 difference. Substituting Q-C gives USD 101000, a USD 3000 difference. These are arithmetic comparisons, not alternative accepted fair values. Averaging all three prices would manufacture a model assumption without resolving the differences in instrument and date.

Now suppose the register contains the same 1000-unit position twice because the exercise stipulates an exact duplicate export. A naive 2000 × 98 gives USD 196000. The duplicate adds USD 98000 to that calculation. In real records, a repeated quantity or ID would not alone establish duplication; verify the position identity and rights before proposing a population change.

## Sensitivity is a question, not a valuation conclusion

For a separate purely mathematical example, suppose an analyst discounts a single certain USD 100 payment due exactly one year after the measurement date using annual compounding. Under those assumptions, 100/1.08 is approximately USD 92.59 and 100/1.10 is approximately USD 90.91. The difference using unrounded results is about USD 1.68.

This example shows how an assumed rate changes a simple formula. It does not establish appropriate cash flows, a market-participant rate, liquidity adjustment, credit model, fair-value hierarchy level or accounting result. Real uncertainty about payment, timing and contractual rights cannot be replaced by the word “certain.” Preserve that exercise assumption explicitly.

## Assemble the source-to-conclusion trail

For each proposed input, write what it measures, why it is relevant and what evidence could contradict its use. Explain any adjustment to an external indication. Ask whether the model reflects the specified instrument, date and purpose. Compare changes in the output with changes in the underlying facts; a large movement deserves explanation even when the formula is unchanged.

For private-asset work, use the SEC statement's staff discussion to prompt questions about delayed information, market changes, valuation inputs and sufficiently specific disclosure. This is not a conclusion that every private asset belongs in one hierarchy level or qualifies for the NAV practical expedient. Those determinations require the applicable current primary requirements and complete facts.
