# Audit assertions: the direction of a test changes what it can show

## The audit idea

Evidence is useful because it addresses a question. Inspecting a document can be persuasive for one assertion and weak for another. A test that begins with recorded items may help examine whether those items are supported, while a completeness test often needs a population outside the ledger whose omissions are being investigated.

The GAO/CIGIE Financial Audit Manual links audit objectives, assertions, risks and procedures in a federal-audit setting, with references to AU-C guidance. It is not a complete verification of the current AICPA standards. The example below is original and does not prescribe a mandatory sample size or a complete audit program.

## Original worked example

A fictional distributor's December purchases ledger contains 240 invoices totaling $480,000. The auditor selects 20 recorded invoices totaling $76,000 and matches them to purchase orders, receiving evidence and supplier invoices. No exception is found among those items.

What has this shown? It provides evidence about the selected recorded purchases. It has not shown that all December receipts were recorded. An omitted invoice could never be selected from a ledger that does not contain it.

For a separate completeness and cutoff procedure, consider 30 independently obtained year-end receiving records totaling $64,000. Two records concern goods received on December 29 and December 30, priced at $6,400 and $3,600. They were recorded only in January. Assume the complete terms establish that the goods and the related obligation belonged in the December accounts.

The identified December omission is $10,000. If both purchases remained unsold at year-end and all other facts are as assumed, an illustrative correction increases inventory and accounts payable by $10,000. It does not necessarily increase December cost of sales. If the goods had already been sold, the income-statement implications would differ.

## Why a “clean” first test does not contradict the second

The populations and assertions differ. The first selection cannot find purchases that are absent from the ledger. The second begins with evidence of receipts and traces toward the accounting records. Both test results can be true at the same time.

Also separate occurrence, existence, rights, valuation and cutoff. A receiving record can support that a delivery happened without proving ownership terms, condition, correct price or classification. Recalculation alone establishes arithmetic, not whether a transaction belongs to this entity.

## A stronger evidence record

Write down the risk, assertion, source population, population reconciliation, selection method, exact procedure, results and follow-up. Keep copies of the evidence or dependable identifiers under the engagement's retention rules. Record contradictory evidence rather than deleting it because another procedure “passed.”

The $10,000 is a known identified omission in this scenario. It is not automatically the total population error, a materiality conclusion or proof of fraud. Further work depends on the cause, extent, risk assessment and professional judgment.
