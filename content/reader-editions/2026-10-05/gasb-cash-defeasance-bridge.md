# Existing-resource debt defeasance: separate the fund payment from the liability exit

## Two reporting views of one transaction

A government can use existing resources to place debt-service funding with an escrow agent. Whether that transaction qualifies for in-substance defeasance must be established separately. A payment confirmation is evidence of cash movement; it is not, by itself, proof that debt can leave the economic-resources statements.

For a qualifying transaction funded solely with existing resources, the selected Texas guidance removes the debt in economic-resources reporting and recognizes the difference between reacquisition price and the adjusted carrying amount as a gain or loss. The governmental-fund view reports the existing-resource payment as a debt-service expenditure. The example below reconciles those effects without issuing any new debt.

## Original scenario and qualification assumption

A fictional county uses accumulated debt-service fund resources to defease an outstanding civic-facilities bond. Assume an appropriate assessment has established all required trust, asset and cash-flow qualifications for in-substance defeasance, including the relevant limitations on future county payments. That conclusion is supplied for this educational calculation; this guide neither interprets the trust agreement nor determines the legal effectiveness of an actual arrangement.

On the transaction date, after all ordinary accruals and amortization through that date, the economic-resources records contain:

- Bond principal of $1,800,000
- An unamortized bond premium of $42,000, with no discount
- A deferred outflow from a prior refunding of $18,000, with no related deferred inflow
- Remaining prepaid insurance attributable to this debt of $6,000

The county pays $1,845,000 to the qualifying escrow using existing cash. Assume that amount is the complete reacquisition price for the example. Exclude separate transaction fees, accrued interest payable, derivative arrangements and other debt-related balances. The old deferred outflow does not mean the current transaction raises new refunding debt: it is a surviving carrying adjustment from a historical transaction.

## Make every carrying adjustment visible

Start with principal plus premium: $1,800,000 plus $42,000 equals a $1,842,000 debt carrying amount before the separate deferred-resource and insurance adjustments. The $18,000 deferred outflow is an amount that must also leave the records. Likewise, the $6,000 prepaid insurance cannot remain as a benefit associated with debt that has been extinguished for reporting purposes.

For this calculation, the adjusted amount against which reacquisition price is compared is $1,818,000: $1,842,000 less $18,000 less $6,000. The $1,845,000 price therefore produces a $27,000 loss. Subtracting only principal would give $45,000, which misses all three carrying adjustments. Comparing price only with principal plus premium would give $3,000, which overlooks the deferred outflow and prepaid insurance.

The signs are easier to understand through the entry than through a memorized label. Removing a premium credit balance needs a debit. Removing a deferred-outflow debit balance and an insurance asset needs credits. Those credits increase the loss needed to balance the transaction. This reasoning also creates an audit trail from each ledger balance to its treatment.

A simplified economic-resources entry debits bonds payable $1,800,000, bond premium $42,000 and loss on defeasance $27,000. It credits cash $1,845,000, the prior-refunding deferred outflow $18,000 and prepaid insurance $6,000. Total debits and credits are each $1,869,000. The loss is separately identified; none of the extinguished balances is left to amortize in future periods under these facts.

## Explain the governmental-fund reconciliation

Assume the governmental debt-service fund carries none of the long-term bond, premium, prior-refunding deferred-outflow or prepaid-insurance balances used above. Its entry is a $1,845,000 debit to debt-service expenditure and a $1,845,000 credit to cash. Its transaction-specific decrease in fund balance is $1,845,000.

The economic-resources change in net position is a $27,000 decrease. Converting the fund result therefore requires a positive $1,818,000 reconciliation adjustment: negative $1,845,000 plus $1,818,000 equals negative $27,000. That adjustment is the combined balance-sheet exit, not new income or another cash receipt.

This bridge prevents a common double count. Recording the entire fund expenditure as an economic-resources expense and then also recording the $27,000 loss would report $1,872,000 of expense for the transaction. The proper conversion replaces the fund's measurement with the economic-resources result; it does not stack the two reporting views together. The associated capital facility and its depreciation are outside this debt-only exercise.

## Changed facts and boundary checks

First, suppose the qualifying reacquisition price is instead $1,811,000, with every carrying balance unchanged. The difference becomes a $7,000 gain because price is below the $1,818,000 adjusted amount. The fund expenditure becomes $1,811,000. The reconciliation adjustment remains positive $1,818,000, producing a positive $7,000 economic-resources result. A gain is possible even though cash leaves the government.

Second, return to the original price but assume the $6,000 prepaid insurance balance had already been fully consumed and properly expensed before the transaction. The adjusted amount becomes $1,824,000 and the transaction loss falls to $21,000. Earlier insurance expense remains in its proper period. This alternative does not authorize writing off insurance twice or ignoring a remaining asset in the original facts.

Finally, if qualifying-defeasance evidence is missing, neither calculation proves that derecognition is allowed. The reporting team must resolve that evidence gap before relying on the liability-exit entry. Keep the signed escrow documents, funding source, transaction-date debt ledger and support for each carrying adjustment together. Subsequent disclosure analysis is separate from deciding whether to retain the bond in this liability calculation.
