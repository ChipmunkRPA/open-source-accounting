## Decide what conclusion the reader needs

A list of observations can answer a tightly specified question without answering a broader assurance question. First identify which outcome the reader needs.

The Louisiana Legislative Auditor's selected guide describes agreed-upon procedures as specified work followed by reported results and exceptions. It describes an examination as obtaining reasonable assurance about subject matter evaluated against criteria and expressing an opinion. This distinction is the source-backed premise here; it does not provide a complete engagement-acceptance checklist. [LLA guide 400-1110, opening descriptions](https://lla.la.gov/resources/local-government-reporting/louisiana-governmental-audit-guide/400-1110-agreed-upon-procedures-and-examination-engagements).

## Turn an operational concern into observable criteria

A fictional shared-workshop operator completed 80 equipment-repair work orders during July through September. The ledger records $64,000 of repair expenditure. Its board wants information about approval timing, recorded charges and documentation of completed work. This original example uses no state checklist or official reporting template.

For a proposed bounded procedures engagement, the parties identify four particular work orders: M104, M118, M133 and M147. These are deliberately named selections, not a statistical sample. The proposed work is to compare three defined attributes for those four items:

1. A written approval is dated on or before the work order's release date. The comparison concerns the document date; it does not authenticate the approver's authority or prove when the work actually began
2. The ledger amount equals the vendor invoice total in dollars and cents. All four stipulated invoices are in one currency and include the full charge; no tax, foreign-exchange or allocation adjustment is needed
3. A signed completion record is dated within five calendar days after documented delivery. Elapsed days exclude the delivery date, include the signature date and do not exclude weekends

These are operational definitions for the exercise. The parties and practitioner would still need to assess whether the proposed procedures and criteria fit an actual engagement. “Looks reasonable” would not specify what to compare or how to record a difference.

## Maintain a bounded actual-results register

Assume the specified records are inspected and yield the following invented observations. Amounts are dollars. The table is an educational workpaper, not a CPA report or prescribed report language.

| Selected order | Ledger | Invoice | Approval compared with release | Completion documentation |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| M104 | $1,440 | $1,440 | July 2 before July 3 | Signed 2 days after delivery |
| M118 | $2,100 | $2,040 | August 9 after August 7 | Signed 3 days after delivery |
| M133 | $960 | $960 | September 2 on September 2 | No signed completion record supplied |
| M147 | $1,920 | $1,920 | September 18 before September 19 | Delivered September 21; signed September 29 |

M104 has no difference under the three specified comparisons. M118 has two: approval is dated two days after release, and the ledger exceeds the invoice by $60. M133 lacks the document needed for the completion-date comparison. M147's eight-day interval exceeds the five-day criterion by three days.

There are four exception observations across three selected work orders. Counting those observations as four distinct deficient orders would double-count M118. Conversely, describing only “three exceptions” would conceal that M118 has two different issues.

The selected ledger amounts total $6,420, while their invoice totals equal $6,360. Their difference is $60. Two of the four selected completion records meet the timing comparison, one is late and one is unavailable. The unavailable document does not establish either timely completion or a particular number of late days.

## Explain exactly what the numbers do not establish

Selected ledger expenditure is $6,420 / $64,000 = 10.03125% of the stated population amount. The four named items are 5% of its stated count. These are descriptive coverage calculations only. Neither establishes representativeness, validates the completeness of the 80-item listing or supports projecting the $60 difference to unexamined items.

The $60 observation also does not automatically authorize a correcting journal entry. The operator should investigate whether it reflects an input error, an omitted invoice or another cause. The procedure compares two records; it does not settle the underlying payable or expense by itself.

Similarly, a dated approval can meet this narrowly defined comparison while other important controls remain untested. No observation here addresses workmanship, vendor conflicts, purchasing authority, hidden duplicate payments or the effectiveness of the whole control system.

## Change the facts without changing the original result silently

Suppose the operator later supplies M133's signed record, dated four calendar days after delivery and supported as contemporaneous. Reperforming the agreed comparison would replace its unavailable-document observation with a timely result. The updated register would have three exception observations across two selected orders, with three timely completion records and one late record.

Retain both versions, the new evidence and the date of the update. A signature newly created after the inspection would require its actual date to be evaluated; it cannot be treated as though it existed earlier. Expanding work to additional orders likewise requires an explicitly defined scope rather than silently converting the four-item register into a broader conclusion.

## When an examination better matches the request

Now change the board's question. It wants reasonable assurance supporting an opinion about specified quarterly repair information measured against agreed criteria. Four named comparisons do not supply that outcome merely because the board calls the work thorough.

An examination would need its own subject-matter boundary, criteria evaluation, evidence strategy and treatment of material misstatement. The practitioner must determine appropriate work for that assurance objective; this guide prescribes neither a sample size nor a clean opinion. Even inspecting every listed order with the same limited comparisons would not automatically create an examination engagement or answer omitted criteria.
