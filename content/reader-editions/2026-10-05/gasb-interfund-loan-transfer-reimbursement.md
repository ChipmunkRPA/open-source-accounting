# Interfund funding: distinguish loans, transfers and reimbursements

## Similar cash movements can carry different meanings

When one fund sends money to another, the bank entry cannot determine whether the recipient owes it back. It also cannot tell whether the payment finances another fund's activities or repays a specific expense initially charged to the wrong fund. Those distinctions determine which balances and flows appear in the fund statements.

Texas Comptroller application guidance distinguishes loans, transfers, interfund services and reimbursements. The example below focuses on two governmental funds of one fictional government. It does not apply proprietary-fund subsidy presentation, where the selected current Texas pages contain inconsistent wording.

## Original scenario: one month, five movements

At the beginning of the month, Fund A has $250,000 cash and Fund B has $40,000 cash. Assume there are no other assets, liabilities or transactions, and that both funds may lawfully participate in the arrangements. Ignore interest and fund-balance constraint classifications; this is a simplified accounting reconciliation, not spending authorization.

Five transactions occur:

1. Fund A lends Fund B $60,000 under an enforceable repayment arrangement. Repayment is reasonably expected
2. Fund A separately sends Fund B $30,000 as a permanent transfer, with no service supplied in return
3. Fund A pays an outside contractor $12,000 for completed work that belongs entirely to Fund B
4. Fund B reimburses Fund A for that exact $12,000 expenditure
5. Fund B repays $15,000 of the loan principal

The $30,000 transfer and $60,000 loan must remain identifiable even if the cash-management system processes them together.

## Follow the entries without combining their purposes

For the loan, Fund A debits an interfund receivable and credits cash by $60,000. Fund B debits cash and credits an interfund payable by the same amount. Neither fund records this borrowing as a transfer or ordinary revenue. The subsequent $15,000 repayment reduces both reciprocal balances, leaving $45,000 due from Fund B in Fund A and $45,000 due to Fund A in Fund B.

For the permanent $30,000 transfer, Fund A records a transfer out and Fund B a transfer in. These are other financing uses and sources in this governmental-fund illustration. They are not receivables and payables waiting for repayment.

For the contractor payment, assume Fund A initially debits expenditure and credits cash by $12,000. The reimbursement then debits Fund A's cash and reduces that expenditure by $12,000. Fund B debits its own expenditure and credits cash by $12,000. After both sides are recorded, the government has one $12,000 expenditure, in Fund B.

This differs from Fund A selling its own services at a price. A reimbursement in this case corrects which fund bears a particular outside cost; it does not create another sale or another transfer.

## Reconcile cash and reciprocal balances

Fund A closes with $175,000 cash:

$250,000 − $60,000 − $30,000 − $12,000 + $12,000 + $15,000 = $175,000.

Fund B closes with $103,000 cash:

$40,000 + $60,000 + $30,000 − $12,000 − $15,000 = $103,000.

Combined fund cash falls from $290,000 to $278,000. The $12,000 decline is the payment to the outside contractor. Internal movements change the distribution of cash without creating additional cash for the government as a whole.

Now include the loan balance. Fund A's simplified ending fund balance is $220,000: $175,000 cash plus its $45,000 receivable. Compared with opening fund balance of $250,000, the decline is $30,000, matching the transfer out.

Fund B's ending fund balance is $58,000: $103,000 cash less its $45,000 payable. Its $18,000 increase equals the $30,000 transfer in less the $12,000 expenditure. Across the funds, the combined change is negative $12,000.

Do not net the $15,000 principal repayment against the $30,000 transfer and report a $15,000 transfer. That would corrupt both the financing-flow presentation and the remaining loan balances.

## Changed fact: the remaining loan becomes nonrepayable

Suppose an authorized decision before the reporting date converts the entire outstanding $45,000 to a permanent transfer. Assume the necessary legal and budget steps have occurred; this is a changed fictional fact, not permission to forgive a real loan.

Fund A removes its $45,000 receivable and records another transfer out. Fund B removes its $45,000 payable and records another transfer in. Neither cash balance changes.

Fund A's ending fund balance becomes $175,000, a $75,000 decline from its opening balance. Fund B's becomes $103,000, a $63,000 increase. The combined change is still negative $12,000: internal reclassification does not undo the outside contractor cost.

Even without a formal conversion, a loan whose repayment is no longer reasonably expected requires reassessment. An old account label cannot substitute for evidence of repayment.
