# New estimate or old error: identify which reporting periods change

**Reader edition 2026-10-05.1** · Wording changes are limited to labels and notices. Earlier check statements refer to the original article revision. No new professional review or source verification is claimed.

**Ray Sang Annotation · Original educational explanation · Version 1.0.0 · 2026-10-04**

## Start with what was known when

A changed number does not tell you whether earlier statements were wrong. A well-supported estimate can change when circumstances change. A spreadsheet can also produce an incorrect number even though the necessary information was available all along. The numerical adjustment and the reason for it must be examined together.

Texas Comptroller application guidance treats estimate revisions prospectively and distinguishes errors involving mistakes or misuse of previously available facts. Its basic-statement correction discussion addresses restating affected prior periods. This case explores that distinction with two separate histories for the same kind of equipment.

Use government-wide economic-resources reporting throughout. Governmental funds do not record this depreciation expense under the case assumptions, so the computations belong in the capital-asset and conversion records rather than the governmental-fund expenditure ledger.

## Original history A: new evidence changes remaining service

A fictional city's environmental laboratory places monitoring equipment in service on January 1 of Year 1. Cost is $144,000; estimated life is eight years; residual value is zero; depreciation is straight-line. Assume these estimates were reasonable when made and remained reasonable through Year 3. Ignore impairment and disposal.

Annual depreciation for each of the first three years is $18,000: $144,000 divided by eight. At December 31 of Year 3, accumulated depreciation is $54,000 and the carrying amount is $90,000.

On January 1 of Year 4, new operating evidence supports six more years of service, rather than the five years remaining under the former estimate. Assume that this is a valid estimate revision, the method stays straight-line, and no earlier facts were overlooked.

Allocate the current $90,000 carrying amount over the revised six-year remaining period. Year 4 depreciation becomes $15,000; the closing carrying amount is $75,000. The reduction from the old annual charge is $3,000.

The entry for Year 4 is a $15,000 debit to depreciation expense and credit to accumulated depreciation. There is no reversal of the $54,000 properly recorded before the change. Dividing the original $144,000 cost by a newly imagined total life and rewriting all earlier years would erase the fact that those estimates were reasonable on their own dates.

## Original history B: the facts were right, the formula was wrong

Consider an alternative history, not an additional event in history A. The original eight-year estimate remains valid. A spreadsheet reference error caused the city to record only $14,000 depreciation in each of Years 1 through 3, although the approved schedule required $18,000. Assume the error is material and is discovered while preparing Year 4 statements with Year 3 comparative information.

The recorded three-year depreciation is $42,000. Correct depreciation is $54,000, so accumulated depreciation is understated and net equipment overstated by $12,000. The incorrect opening Year 4 carrying amount of $102,000 must become $90,000.

Separate the correction by the periods presented:

- Years 1 and 2 each have a $4,000 understatement of expense. Their combined $8,000 effect reduces beginning Year 3 net position
- Year 3 depreciation is restated upward by $4,000, from $14,000 to $18,000
- Those components total the $12,000 reduction in ending Year 3 net position and opening Year 4 net position
- Year 4's ordinary depreciation is $18,000, leaving $72,000 net equipment at its end

A ledger-level opening correction debits beginning net position and credits accumulated depreciation by $12,000, subject to the reporting system's restatement mechanics. Presentation of comparative Year 3 then allocates that total between its $8,000 opening effect and $4,000 period effect. These are two descriptions of one correction, not three additional charges.

Charging $30,000 to Year 4 depreciation by combining $18,000 normal expense and the $12,000 old error would conceal the periods affected. The corrected comparative information and the current-year expense answer different questions.

## Changed fact: shorter remaining service

Return to history A. Suppose the new evidence instead supports only three remaining years, with no impairment and no residual value. The prospective charge becomes $30,000: $90,000 divided by three. Year 4 closes at $60,000.

A larger expense does not make the revision an error correction. Conversely, calling an old spreadsheet mistake an “estimate update” does not make it prospective. The information chronology determines the classification; the direction of the financial effect does not.

## Explain the change to a reader

For history A, identify the new evidence, effective revision date, affected expense and asset lines, and significance of the change. For history B, explain the erroneous calculation, affected periods and the bridge from previously reported to corrected balances. These are different explanations even when both pass an arithmetic check.
