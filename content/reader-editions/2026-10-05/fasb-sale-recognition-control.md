# Selling a nonfinancial asset: separate the bank deposit from the accounting sale

## The accounting idea

Receiving a deposit and completing a sale are distinct events. An asset-sale analysis asks what contract exists, whether the relevant recognition conditions are met and when control passes. A cash receipt alone cannot settle those questions.

OCC's August 2026 Bank Accounting Advisory Series, section 5A, discusses ASC 606 contract/control questions in the context of bank-owned real estate and ASC 610-20. It is an interpretive banking source. The smaller equipment scenario below teaches the timing logic under explicit assumptions; it is not an application of every real-estate rule to equipment.

## Original worked example

A company intends to sell a retired machine outside its ordinary product sales. Its carrying amount is $42,000 and the agreed cash price is $55,000. On December 28, the buyer pays a refundable $5,000 deposit. The machine remains at the seller's premises, the seller can still redirect it, and a substantive acceptance condition remains unresolved. Assume those facts mean no accounting sale has occurred by December 31.

At December 31, the teaching entry is a $5,000 debit to cash and a $5,000 credit to a deposit liability. No $13,000 disposal gain is recognized, and the machine remains recorded at its carrying amount, subject to any separate impairment or held-for-sale analysis.

On January 10, the condition is satisfied, the remaining $50,000 is paid and the buyer obtains control. Assume a complete analysis supports recognition on that date and there are no selling costs, financing effects or further obligations. The entry is:

- Debit cash $50,000
- Debit deposit liability $5,000
- Credit machine carrying amount $42,000
- Credit disposal gain $13,000

Debits and credits both total $55,000. Across both dates, total cash collected is $55,000; the gain is only $13,000. Calling all cash received revenue would mix proceeds with profit and disregard the asset removed.

## How changing facts changes the answer

If the remaining amount is financed over several years, the stated price may no longer be the appropriate measurement without considering financing. If the machine is sold in the company's ordinary activities, the reporting model and income-statement presentation need fresh analysis. If the seller must repurchase it, guarantee outcomes or provide significant ongoing services, the simple disposal scenario may no longer describe the arrangement.

Also distinguish physical delivery from control. A warehouse location, invoice date or title clause is one piece of evidence. A reviewer should understand who can direct use, who is entitled to the benefits and which contractual conditions remain.

## Close-file checklist

Retain the contract, acceptance evidence, deposit terms, payment dates, asset register and approval of any other balance-sheet measurement. Record the specific unresolved condition at year-end rather than an unsupported conclusion that the transaction is merely “pending.” The example answers only the stipulated recognition timing; it is not a complete disclosure checklist.
