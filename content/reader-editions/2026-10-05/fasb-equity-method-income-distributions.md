# Equity-method investments: income and cash distributions move different balances

## Start after the eligibility decision

An ownership percentage is not a complete accounting model. This exercise stipulates that a separate, supported assessment has already established equity-method accounting. It neither establishes significant influence nor decides consolidation, joint-venture status or another scope exception.

The selected Federal Reserve instructions describe an investment balance adjusted for the investor's share of earnings or losses and reduced for cash distributions. The teaching problem is to keep those two movements separate. Cash arriving from an investee does not automatically represent additional income for an investor that has already recognized its share of the underlying earnings.

## Original facts and deliberate omissions

Fictional investor Meadow acquires a 35% interest for $210,000 at the beginning of a reporting year. Its share of the investee's relevant underlying carrying amounts equals that purchase price. Assume the equity-method determination is valid throughout both periods, financial information is timely and comparable, and the distribution rights match the stated proportion.

Exclude acquisition-basis differences, intercompany profit, preferred returns, investee OCI, impairment, loss-recognition limits, tax, foreign currency and changes in ownership. Each would require additional analysis rather than an unexplained plug to the simple rollforward.

During the first period, the investee earns $84,000 and pays total cash distributions of $20,000. During the second, it incurs a $16,000 loss and distributes another $8,000. The distribution amounts are totals paid by the investee to all owners. They are not amounts paid solely to Meadow.

## Calculate ownership shares once

Meadow's first-period earnings share is 35% × $84,000 = $29,400. Its distribution is 35% × $20,000 = $7,000. The second-period loss share is 35% × $16,000 = $5,600, and its second distribution is 35% × $8,000 = $2,800.

| Investment rollforward | First period | Second period |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening investment | $210,000 | $232,400 |
| Share of earnings or loss | $29,400 | $(5,600) |
| Cash distribution received | $(7,000) | $(2,800) |
| Closing investment | $232,400 | $224,000 |

At acquisition, debit investment $210,000 and credit cash $210,000. To record the first-period earnings, debit investment $29,400 and credit equity-method income $29,400. On receipt of the distribution, debit cash $7,000 and credit investment $7,000.

In the second period, debit equity-method loss $5,600 and credit investment $5,600. The cash distribution entry separately debits cash $2,800 and credits investment $2,800. The investor does not ignore the loss simply because it also received cash during that period.

The entries are illustrative under the stated assumptions. Their balance does not prove that the investee's reported income is reliable or that every distribution has the same legal or accounting character.

## Prove that cash and income reconcile

Across both periods, recognized net income from the investment is $29,400 minus $5,600 = $23,800. Cash received is $7,000 plus $2,800 = $9,800. Their difference, $14,000, equals the investment balance increase from $210,000 to $224,000.

The independent total-return bridge is ending investment $224,000 plus cash received $9,800 minus original investment $210,000 = $23,800. The bridge works because the assumptions exclude other investment movements; it is not a universal return formula for a complex equity-method file.

A common incorrect approach records the $9,800 distributions as additional income while leaving them out of the investment rollforward. That approach reports $33,600 income and a $233,800 investment balance. Each exceeds the correct result by $9,800. The cash balance can still reconcile, so reviewing only the bank receipts would miss the double counting.

## Change the distribution without changing the earnings

Suppose the investee's second-period distribution were $20,000 instead of $8,000, with all other assumptions unchanged. Meadow would receive $7,000 in that period, an additional $4,200. Its closing investment would fall from $224,000 to $219,800; total cash distributions would rise from $9,800 to $14,000.

Recognized net income would remain $23,800. Ending investment $219,800 plus cash $14,000 minus initial investment $210,000 still equals that amount. A larger distribution changes where the investor's recognized value sits; it does not create another share of investee earnings.

A different variation, such as losses exceeding the available investment balance or an obligation to support the investee, falls outside the illustration. Do not simply extend the table below zero without the relevant model analysis.

## Evidence and precise source

Retain the separate eligibility assessment, ownership chronology, investee financial statements, distribution resolutions, receipt records and basis-difference analysis. Reconcile the investee-wide amounts to the investor's shares, and explain every other movement before accepting the closing investment.
