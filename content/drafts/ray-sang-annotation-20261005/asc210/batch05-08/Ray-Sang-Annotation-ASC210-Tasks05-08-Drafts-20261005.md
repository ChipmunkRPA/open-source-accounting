## ASC 210 lesson 5: separate a long-term restricted cash fund from current resources

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

### Focused question

When does a long-term restriction keep a cash balance out of current resources?

### One concept

The word cash describes the asset's form. It does not establish that the money is available for ordinary current operations. A binding restriction can reserve a fund for a long-term purpose or prevent access beyond the applicable current-classification horizon. The restriction's purpose and release conditions therefore need examination before the balance is grouped with current resources.

For a limited example of actual reporting practice, [Fluence Energy's March 31, 2026 filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1868941/000110465926056304/flnc-20260331x10q.htm) describes noncurrent treatment for restricted funds associated with long-term uses or extended unavailability. That is the company's disclosed policy, not SEC approval or a complete authoritative rule for every entity. The fictional illustration below does not reproduce its transactions or amounts.

### Small fictional example

Fictional Amber Kite Services has $37,000 in an unrestricted operating account and $63,000 in a separate escrow account at December 31, Year 1. Under the stipulated enforceable escrow terms, the second account can be used only to repay principal due on March 31, Year 4. Amber Kite cannot withdraw or redirect it before that date. Assume a one-year classification horizon, a continuing ordinary reporting basis and no special provision that changes the conclusion.

The $63,000 fund serves the long-term repayment purpose and is unavailable for current operations under these facts. It is presented as a noncurrent restricted asset in this simplified example. The $37,000 operating balance remains current. The classification split preserves $100,000 of total cash assets; it does not create a loss or remove the escrow balance from the entity's assets.

If Amber Kite has $18,000 of other current assets, the stated balances produce $55,000 of current assets: $37,000 plus $18,000. Including the escrow in that subtotal would produce $118,000, overstating current assets by $63,000. Total assets would be unchanged by that classification error, assuming the escrow was counted only once.

The escrow does not disappear against the borrowing merely because both relate to the same repayment. Any permission to offset an asset and liability is a separate question, outside this lesson.

Change the facts: management merely names an ordinary bank account Future Projects and can freely redirect its funds. The account name alone does not establish the binding restriction assumed above. Conversely, a restricted fund intended to settle a current obligation is not automatically noncurrent merely because a restriction exists. Each case requires its own purpose, timing and applicable-guidance analysis.

### Common mistake

Treating every bank balance as available current cash, or treating every restricted balance as noncurrent. Either shortcut replaces the actual conditions with an account label.

### Check the decision

Would a proposed request to release the escrow make the $63,000 currently available if the required approval has not been obtained?

No, under the stated terms. A hoped-for change is not the same as an effective release. If circumstances later change, the facts and the relevant reporting-date requirements need reassessment.

## ASC 210 lesson 6: classify an operating payable using its normal settlement cycle

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

### Focused question

Why can an ordinary operating payable be current even when its settlement is part of a longer normal operating cycle?

### One concept

An operating payable arises from the ordinary resource-to-cash process, such as obtaining materials on supplier credit. Its current classification can reflect that normal operating cycle when the cycle is longer than a year. This is different from mechanically classifying every obligation only by whether twelve months have passed. The payable's nature, actual terms and relationship to the cycle remain important.

As a limited reporting-practice observation, [Northrop Grumman's June 30, 2026 policy disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1133421/000113342126000034/R18.htm) uses customer-contract duration in defining a cycle longer than one year for certain current assets and liabilities. That observation supports asking about the cycle. It does not establish the result for an unrelated company, every payable or a borrowing.

### Small fictional example

Fictional Willow Arc Installations undertakes custom fit-out projects. Assume a documented twenty-month normal operating cycle, an ordinary $28,000 trade payable for materials already received, and payment due fourteen months after the reporting date under customary terms for these projects. The payable will be settled with resources generated in that same normal operating cycle. Assume the supported carrying amount is $28,000 and there is no unusual financing, modification or specialized provision affecting classification.

Under this simplified operating-cycle analysis, the fourteen-month timing does not by itself make the payable noncurrent. It falls inside the stipulated twenty-month ordinary cycle. Presenting the $28,000 as current communicates that relationship. It does not mean payment is contractually due today or that the supplier can demand earlier settlement under facts not stated here.

Now add $7,000 of ordinary operating invoices due within sixty days. The two groups total $35,000 of current trade payables under the assumptions: $28,000 plus $7,000. Moving the first group out merely because fourteen months exceeds one year would understate that current subtotal by $28,000. No amount has been extinguished or forgiven.

Next change the first payable's actual contract: it is due in sixty days, but management expects to pay late, after fourteen months. A plan to miss the deadline does not amend the contract or create a longer normal operating cycle. The existing payment obligation remains relevant. Any subsequent renegotiation, default consequence or financing arrangement needs separate assessment.

The normal cycle must describe operations, not a period chosen to accommodate whichever unpaid invoice is oldest. Evidence could include typical production, delivery and collection timelines. One stalled project is not enough, by itself, to establish a new normal cycle.

### Common mistake

Assuming that current means payable within exactly one year in every operating context, or that a current classification grants permission to postpone the contract's due date.

### Check the decision

Returning to the original fourteen-month contractual due date, must the current $28,000 payable be budgeted as due within the next sixty days?

No. In the original example, the cash budget should use the fourteen-month contractual due date. In the changed example, it should identify the sixty-day contractual obligation and any expected late payment separately; management’s intention to pay after fourteen months does not remove the earlier cash need. Current classification does not replace either contractual schedule.

### Cross-references and limits

[Lesson 8](content/guides/asc210-08-working-capital-availability-v0.1.md) shows why balance-sheet categories do not replace dated cash forecasts. Earlier ASC 210 lesson 4 addresses installment principal on a borrowing; this example is confined to an ordinary operating trade payable.

This is an editorial ASC 210-10 association, not verified paragraph mapping. The twenty-month cycle and ordinary supplier terms are assumptions, not established industry facts. Debt classification, supplier-finance programs, covenant breaches, refinancing, demand obligations and discounting require their own analysis. No conclusion about those matters is implied.

## ASC 210 lesson 7: identify the current service obligation behind a customer advance

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

### Focused question

How can a current obligation be settled by providing a service rather than paying cash?

### One concept

A liability need not require a cash refund. It can represent an obligation to provide goods or services already paid for. The [SEC's introductory financial-statement guide](https://www.sec.gov/reportspubs/investor-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguidehtm.html) includes future customer performance among the kinds of obligations reported as liabilities. That elementary description does not establish recognition or measurement for a particular customer contract.

Once a customer advance has been appropriately recognized, a classification analysis considers when the entity expects to satisfy the related obligation and the applicable requirements. Calling an advance nonrefundable does not, by itself, show that performance is complete or that the remaining obligation is noncurrent.

### Small fictional example

Fictional Moss Lantern Studio receives $30,000 on December 31, Year 1 for fifteen equal monthly design-support service periods beginning January 1, Year 2. For this exercise, a separate supported revenue analysis establishes one continuing service obligation, equal satisfaction over the fifteen months and an initial $30,000 contract liability. Assume a one-year classification horizon and no refund rights, variable consideration, financing component or other terms that alter this simplified example.

Each month's allocated service amount is $2,000: $30,000 divided by fifteen. The first twelve months correspond to $24,000 of the liability. The last three months correspond to $6,000. Under the stipulated schedule, the balance-sheet presentation at December 31 separates $24,000 current and $6,000 noncurrent.

Both portions remain obligations at that date because no service has yet been provided. The split does not create revenue, increase cash again or double the liability. The two portions reconcile to the $30,000 already received.

When January's assumed service is completed, the illustrative liability decreases by $2,000 and $2,000 of revenue is recognized under the stipulated revenue analysis. The service performance settles that part of the obligation. It need not involve returning $2,000 to the customer. Providing the service may still require wages and other spending; the absence of a refund obligation does not make performance costless.

Change the promised service pattern: a substantial part of the fee now pays for a separately identifiable deliverable in month fifteen. Dividing the receipt by fifteen is no longer justified merely by the number of months. The recognition, allocation and performance analysis must be revisited before the current portion is calculated. This lesson does not decide the revised contract's result.

### Common mistake

Treating the entire receipt as immediate revenue because it is nonrefundable, or describing the current contract-liability amount as an unavoidable cash refund when the actual obligation is service performance.

### Check the decision

Would spending the advance on equipment eliminate Moss Lantern's remaining obligation to its customer?

No. Using the cash does not supply the promised services. Under the stated facts, performance determines how the assumed liability is satisfied. The equipment purchase has its own accounting.

### Cross-references and limits

[Lesson 6](content/guides/asc210-06-operating-trade-payables-v0.1.md) deals with cash settlement of a supplier obligation. Earlier ASC 210 lesson 2 examines a purchaser's prepaid benefit; this lesson examines the service provider's liability. The two examples do not imply that counterparties always use identical recognition models.

This draft is associated editorially with ASC 210-10 and intersects revenue guidance. Actual recognition, contract allocation, refunds, cancellation rights and changes in performance timing require current analysis. No full revenue-recognition test or tax treatment is provided.

## ASC 210 lesson 8: test available cash separately from positive working capital

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

### Focused question

Does positive working capital show enough spendable cash for a particular payment date?

### One concept

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The [SEC's introductory guide](https://www.sec.gov/reportspubs/investor-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguidehtm.html) describes this subtraction and separately explains cash-flow information. A positive result is a balance-sheet measure, not a schedule showing that cash will be available whenever each payment becomes due.

The current-asset total can include receivables awaiting collection, inventory awaiting sale and benefits that will be consumed rather than converted back to cash. A liability can fall due before those resources generate usable cash. A payment-date check therefore needs the amounts, timing and actual availability of the relevant cash, not only the two current subtotals.

### Small fictional example

Fictional Cobalt Nest Repairs reports these assumed correctly recognized and classified balances at December 31, Year 1: $8,000 of unrestricted cash, $32,000 of collectible receivables, $24,000 of inventory and $6,000 of prepaid services. Its current assets total $70,000. Current liabilities total $40,000, so working capital is positive $30,000.

The $40,000 consists of $22,000 due on January 15, Year 2 and $18,000 due on March 31. Assume $10,000 of the receivables will be collected by January 15 and the remaining $22,000 in February. The inventory generates no cash before January 15, the prepaid services cannot be refunded, and there are no other receipts, payments or financing sources before that first payment date.

Available cash for January 15 is $18,000: opening cash of $8,000 plus collections of $10,000. The $22,000 obligation therefore exceeds the stipulated cash available by $4,000. The positive $30,000 working-capital figure has not prevented this particular timing gap.

The initial current-asset total reconciles: $8,000 plus $32,000 plus $24,000 plus $6,000 equals $70,000. That reconciliation verifies the subtotal, not the receipt dates. Adding $30,000 of working capital to the $18,000 available cash would count overlapping balance-sheet resources again rather than identify a new source of funds.

Now change only one assumption: an additional $4,000 of the receivables is collected by January 15 rather than in February. Available cash rises to $22,000, exactly covering the stated obligation. The reporting-date working-capital total is unchanged. What changed is the collection timing used in the cash schedule. An unsupported hope of early collection would not establish this alternative outcome.

### Common mistake

Treating working capital as a spare cash account, or treating inventory and prepayments as immediately available money without establishing how and when they can meet a payment.

### Check the decision

Does the $4,000 gap alone establish that Cobalt Nest must liquidate or that substantial doubt exists about going concern?

No. It establishes the result of this narrow, stipulated cash schedule. A real assessment would consider the complete facts, relevant plans, probabilities, reporting requirements and applicable period. Those broader conclusions are outside this lesson.
