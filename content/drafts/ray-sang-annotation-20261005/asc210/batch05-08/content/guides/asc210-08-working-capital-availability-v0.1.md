# ASC 210 lesson 8: test available cash separately from positive working capital

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

## Focused question

Does positive working capital show enough spendable cash for a particular payment date?

## One concept

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. The [SEC's introductory guide](https://www.sec.gov/reportspubs/investor-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguidehtm.html) describes this subtraction and separately explains cash-flow information. A positive result is a balance-sheet measure, not a schedule showing that cash will be available whenever each payment becomes due.

The current-asset total can include receivables awaiting collection, inventory awaiting sale and benefits that will be consumed rather than converted back to cash. A liability can fall due before those resources generate usable cash. A payment-date check therefore needs the amounts, timing and actual availability of the relevant cash, not only the two current subtotals.

## Small fictional example

Fictional Cobalt Nest Repairs reports these assumed correctly recognized and classified balances at December 31, Year 1: $8,000 of unrestricted cash, $32,000 of collectible receivables, $24,000 of inventory and $6,000 of prepaid services. Its current assets total $70,000. Current liabilities total $40,000, so working capital is positive $30,000.

The $40,000 consists of $22,000 due on January 15, Year 2 and $18,000 due on March 31. Assume $10,000 of the receivables will be collected by January 15 and the remaining $22,000 in February. The inventory generates no cash before January 15, the prepaid services cannot be refunded, and there are no other receipts, payments or financing sources before that first payment date.

Available cash for January 15 is $18,000: opening cash of $8,000 plus collections of $10,000. The $22,000 obligation therefore exceeds the stipulated cash available by $4,000. The positive $30,000 working-capital figure has not prevented this particular timing gap.

The initial current-asset total reconciles: $8,000 plus $32,000 plus $24,000 plus $6,000 equals $70,000. That reconciliation verifies the subtotal, not the receipt dates. Adding $30,000 of working capital to the $18,000 available cash would count overlapping balance-sheet resources again rather than identify a new source of funds.

Now change only one assumption: an additional $4,000 of the receivables is collected by January 15 rather than in February. Available cash rises to $22,000, exactly covering the stated obligation. The reporting-date working-capital total is unchanged. What changed is the collection timing used in the cash schedule. An unsupported hope of early collection would not establish this alternative outcome.

## Common mistake

Treating working capital as a spare cash account, or treating inventory and prepayments as immediately available money without establishing how and when they can meet a payment.

## Check the decision

Does the $4,000 gap alone establish that Cobalt Nest must liquidate or that substantial doubt exists about going concern?

No. It establishes the result of this narrow, stipulated cash schedule. A real assessment would consider the complete facts, relevant plans, probabilities, reporting requirements and applicable period. Those broader conclusions are outside this lesson.
