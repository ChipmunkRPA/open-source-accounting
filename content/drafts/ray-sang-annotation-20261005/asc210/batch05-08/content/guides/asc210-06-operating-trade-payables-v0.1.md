# ASC 210 lesson 6: classify an operating payable using its normal settlement cycle

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

## Focused question

Why can an ordinary operating payable be current even when its settlement is part of a longer normal operating cycle?

## One concept

An operating payable arises from the ordinary resource-to-cash process, such as obtaining materials on supplier credit. Its current classification can reflect that normal operating cycle when the cycle is longer than a year. This is different from mechanically classifying every obligation only by whether twelve months have passed. The payable's nature, actual terms and relationship to the cycle remain important.

As a limited reporting-practice observation, [Northrop Grumman's June 30, 2026 policy disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1133421/000113342126000034/R18.htm) uses customer-contract duration in defining a cycle longer than one year for certain current assets and liabilities. That observation supports asking about the cycle. It does not establish the result for an unrelated company, every payable or a borrowing.

## Small fictional example

Fictional Willow Arc Installations undertakes custom fit-out projects. Assume a documented twenty-month normal operating cycle, an ordinary $28,000 trade payable for materials already received, and payment due fourteen months after the reporting date under customary terms for these projects. The payable will be settled with resources generated in that same normal operating cycle. Assume the supported carrying amount is $28,000 and there is no unusual financing, modification or specialized provision affecting classification.

Under this simplified operating-cycle analysis, the fourteen-month timing does not by itself make the payable noncurrent. It falls inside the stipulated twenty-month ordinary cycle. Presenting the $28,000 as current communicates that relationship. It does not mean payment is contractually due today or that the supplier can demand earlier settlement under facts not stated here.

Now add $7,000 of ordinary operating invoices due within sixty days. The two groups total $35,000 of current trade payables under the assumptions: $28,000 plus $7,000. Moving the first group out merely because fourteen months exceeds one year would understate that current subtotal by $28,000. No amount has been extinguished or forgiven.

Next change the first payable's actual contract: it is due in sixty days, but management expects to pay late, after fourteen months. A plan to miss the deadline does not amend the contract or create a longer normal operating cycle. The existing payment obligation remains relevant. Any subsequent renegotiation, default consequence or financing arrangement needs separate assessment.

The normal cycle must describe operations, not a period chosen to accommodate whichever unpaid invoice is oldest. Evidence could include typical production, delivery and collection timelines. One stalled project is not enough, by itself, to establish a new normal cycle.

## Common mistake

Assuming that current means payable within exactly one year in every operating context, or that a current classification grants permission to postpone the contract's due date.

## Check the decision

Returning to the original fourteen-month contractual due date, must the current $28,000 payable be budgeted as due within the next sixty days?

No. In the original example, the cash budget should use the fourteen-month contractual due date. In the changed example, it should identify the sixty-day contractual obligation and any expected late payment separately; management’s intention to pay after fourteen months does not remove the earlier cash need. Current classification does not replace either contractual schedule.
