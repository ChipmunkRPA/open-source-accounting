# ASC 210 lesson 2: split a prepayment by when its benefit will be consumed

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Draft reader edition 0.1.1 · October 5, 2026**

Original educational draft. Not professionally reviewed. Current authoritative requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. Topic and lesson numbers organize this reading series; lesson numbers are not ASC paragraph numbers.

## Focused question

Can an asset be current even when it will provide a service rather than turn back into cash?

## One concept

A recognized prepaid operating benefit can qualify as current through consumption within the relevant classification horizon. It need not produce a cash refund. As the benefit is consumed, the unconsumed asset declines and the related cost is recognized in the appropriate expense or other account. [OpenStax's explanation of prepaid-expense adjustments](https://openstax.org/books/principles-financial-accounting/pages/4-2-discuss-the-adjustment-process-and-illustrate-common-types-of-adjusting-entries) supports this basic asset-to-consumption relationship; it is not verification of current ASC requirements.

The classification question starts with a supported asset balance. It does not answer whether a payment should have been capitalized, which measurement basis applies, or whether an impairment or refund changes that balance. Those issues come first when they are unresolved.

## Small fictional example

Fictional Copper Finch Studio prepays $21,600 for eighteen months of routine equipment servicing beginning January 1, Year 2. Its balance-sheet date is December 31, Year 1, before any service starts. Assume the agreement creates an appropriately recognized prepaid service asset, services are provided evenly, and there are no additional deliverables, financing effects or impairment concerns. Also assume a one-year classification horizon applies to this business.

The monthly benefit is $1,200: $21,600 divided by eighteen. Twelve months of benefit, or $14,400, will be consumed in Year 2. The remaining six months, or $7,200, will be consumed in Year 3. Under those stipulated facts, the presentation splits the recognized prepayment into $14,400 current and $7,200 noncurrent.

The split preserves the total $21,600 asset. It creates no new expense on December 31 merely because part of the asset is classified as current. When January's service is provided, the example's assumed even consumption pattern would reduce the prepaid asset by $1,200 and recognize $1,200 of servicing expense. The classification decision and the consumption entry answer different questions.

Suppose instead that the last six months cover a separate intensive overhaul priced differently from routine monthly service. Dividing the total equally by eighteen would no longer be a justified assumption. The preparer would need a supported allocation and consumption pattern before calculating either portion. The calendar alone cannot supply the missing measurement analysis.

## Common mistake

Calling the entire prepayment noncurrent because no cash will be refunded. The expected benefit is service consumption. Another mistake is treating the entire amount as a current-year expense because cash was paid before year end.

## Check the decision

If all eighteen months of service remain unprovided at the reporting date, should classifying $14,400 as current reduce total assets to $7,200?

No. The two classification portions still add to $21,600. Expense recognition follows the supported consumption pattern, not the word current in the balance-sheet caption.
