# ASC 105 foundational lesson 6: evaluate an error in context before calling it immaterial

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

Materiality helps determine whether an omission or misstatement matters to users of the financial statements. The analysis considers the amount and nature of the matter in context, including its combination with other matters. A mechanical percentage can be a useful starting point, but it does not supply a universal safe harbor.

An individually small error may matter because it changes a key trend, masks a loss, affects a contractual threshold, involves an important disclosure or forms part of a larger pattern. Conversely, identifying a possible qualitative factor does not automatically complete the assessment. The preparer must evaluate what the factor means for the statements and their users.

Materiality is not permission to design a misleading accounting result. Deliberate efforts to reach a target warrant particular scrutiny even when the proposed adjustment looks small in relation to one benchmark.

## Work through several errors together

Fictional Alder Components reports draft pretax profit of $960,000. Its close review finds four expense accruals omitted from the same period: $8,000, $11,000, $17,000 and $24,000. For this exercise, all four errors overstate pretax profit, there are no tax effects, and no other errors have yet been identified.

Individually, the omitted expenses range from approximately 0.83% to 2.50% of draft pretax profit. Together they total $60,000, or 6.25% of that draft benchmark. Corrected pretax profit would be $900,000.

These calculations show why reviewing each item in isolation can miss the combined effect. They do not establish a universal 6.25% decision rule. The denominator is also explicit: using corrected pretax profit instead would produce approximately 6.67%. Choosing the denominator that makes an error look smallest is not a substitute for a justified benchmark.

Add a stipulated fact: a significant lending covenant uses a profit measure that, for this exercise only, exactly equals pretax profit and requires at least $925,000. Draft profit exceeds that amount by $35,000. Corrected profit is below it by $25,000. That consequence is important to the assessment. The calculation does not determine the real legal consequences of a covenant breach, which would depend on the agreement and other facts.

## Keep netting and classification questions visible

Suppose a separate revenue error understates profit by $15,000. The net profit overstatement would fall to $45,000. That net figure does not erase the $60,000 expense understatement or the $15,000 revenue understatement. Effects on individual line items, relevant disclosures and other periods may still matter.

Similarly, moving an amount between two expense lines can leave total profit unchanged while affecting important information presented to users. Zero net profit effect is a useful fact, not a complete materiality conclusion.

The file should show the individual amounts, how they combine, the benchmarks considered and the reasons a qualitative factor matters. It should also identify missing information. In an actual engagement, management, auditors and other responsible reviewers have their own roles; this lesson does not supply a professional judgment or a filing decision.

## Check your understanding

May Alder call all four accrual omissions immaterial solely because each is below 3% of draft pretax profit?

No. That ignores aggregation, the stipulated covenant effect and the absence of any established universal 3% rule. The correct conclusion requires the complete context and applicable requirements.
