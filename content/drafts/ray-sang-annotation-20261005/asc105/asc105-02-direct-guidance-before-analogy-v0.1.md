# ASC 105 foundational lesson 2: apply direct guidance before choosing an analogy

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

A transaction's accounting treatment starts with guidance that actually covers it. When directly applicable guidance supplies an answer, a more attractive analogy does not create a free choice of accounting methods. The relevant guidance may include a general model, an industry provision and an exception; applicability must be resolved before the conclusion is chosen.

Direct application means applying the requirements governing this transaction to its facts. Analogy means considering a treatment developed for a different but sufficiently similar transaction when the accounting question is not resolved directly. Those routes carry different evidential burdens.

Difficulty finding the guidance is not proof that no guidance exists. Nor is an unfamiliar commercial label proof that the transaction is new for accounting purposes. A “digital access pass,” for example, may contain obligations or rights that fit established accounting categories even though that marketing label does not appear in a search result.

## Work through a direct-guidance conflict

Fictional Harbor Equipment receives a $42,000 customer payment. A junior preparer proposes recognizing it immediately because the agreement calls the payment a “nonrefundable activation contribution.” A manager suggests copying the treatment used by a trade association for member dues.

Neither the payment's name nor the other entity's published policy establishes Harbor's answer. Harbor must first determine what it promised, who its customer is, which enforceable rights and obligations exist, and which accounting requirements cover the arrangement. Nonrefundability is one contract fact; it does not by itself prove that all required performance has occurred.

Assume, solely to isolate the teaching point, that a complete analysis establishes a directly applicable model under which the entire amount is earned evenly over fourteen months and nothing is earned before that service begins. The resulting monthly amount is $3,000: $42,000 ÷ 14. The association's immediate recognition policy cannot displace that stipulated conclusion merely because management prefers it.

Now change the facts. Suppose the $42,000 represents several distinguishable promises and includes an amount collected for another party. The fourteen-month arithmetic is no longer enough. The accounting question must be reopened because the facts supporting the assumed model changed. This is not an invitation to select whichever analogy produces the desired total.

## Resolve scope before comparing outcomes

Ask what each candidate piece of guidance governs. Does it address this reporting entity, this kind of right or obligation, and this stage of the transaction? Does a more specific provision modify the general treatment? Is there an explicit exception? A narrow provision can be decisive, but its apparent specificity does not make it applicable outside its stated scope.

Only after that work can the preparer describe a genuine gap. An unresolved scope question should remain an unresolved scope question. Labeling it “no guidance” prematurely hides the very issue that could control the answer.

## Check your understanding

Management finds two educational articles with different accounting outcomes. Does that create a permitted policy election?

No. Different articles may describe different facts, different reporting periods, different entity categories or an error. A permitted election must be established under the applicable requirements; disagreement among examples does not create one.
