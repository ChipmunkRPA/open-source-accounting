# ASC 205 presentation lesson 6: set the going-concern evaluation period before testing the forecast

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

A going-concern evaluation is management's assessment of whether conditions and events, taken together, raise substantial doubt about the entity's ability to meet its obligations as they become due. In the model described here, substantial doubt concerns a probable inability to meet those obligations within the relevant period. It is not limited to checking whether year-end net assets are positive or whether last year's operations produced a profit.

The model described here uses a one-year look-forward period from the date the financial statements are issued or available to be issued, as applicable. The applicable date depends on the entity and reporting circumstances; management does not freely choose whichever date produces the shortest forecast. A forecast ending one year after the balance-sheet date may therefore stop too soon.

The initial evaluation considers the relevant conditions and events in aggregate, using information known and reasonably knowable at the applicable assessment date. It does not give credit in that first step for management plans that have not been fully implemented. If that evaluation raises substantial doubt, the separate analysis of mitigating plans follows. Keeping the two stages visible prevents an uncommitted future rescue from disappearing inside the base case.

## Work through a maturity that falls outside the first draft

Fictional Linden Analytics has a December 31, Year 1 balance-sheet date. For this exercise, assume its financial statements are available to be issued on March 20, Year 2 and that this is the applicable date for the evaluation. The one-year look-forward period therefore extends to March 20, Year 3.

The treasury team initially prepares a forecast only through December 31, Year 2. That forecast appears to cover all routine operating payments. However, a substantial loan is due on January 15, Year 3. The maturity falls after the team's first forecast ends but before the proper look-forward period ends.

Leaving the loan out would not be a harmless formatting choice. It would remove an obligation within the evaluation period. The team needs to extend the forecast, include the maturity and relevant operating needs, and assess the resulting conditions together. If the company relies on renewing the loan, the renewal is also an important fact to analyze under the separate management-plans step.

Now assume a major customer's financial failure becomes known on February 25, Year 2, before the statements are available to be issued. That information could affect collections and future cash receipts used in the evaluation. Its treatment in the recorded year-end balances is a related but distinct subsequent-events question. A preparer should not ignore it in the going-concern assessment simply because it occurred after December 31.

## Evaluate liquidity when obligations are due

A full-year forecast total can conceal a cash shortage in a particular month. Cash arriving in March cannot settle a January obligation unless a valid financing or other arrangement bridges the interval. The timing, restrictions and availability of cash matter alongside the annual totals.

Likewise, positive equity is not the same as accessible liquidity. Valuable equipment might support the balance sheet without being available for sale in time, or without being sellable while essential operations continue. The forecast should connect actual obligations with resources available when needed.

The substantial-doubt assessment is a probability-based accounting judgment, not a guarantee that failure will occur and not a generic warning about every commercial risk. Specific conditions, aggregation, timing and the applicable threshold need to be documented. This introductory lesson does not supply a numerical probability cutoff.

## Check your understanding

May Linden stop at December 31, Year 2 because that is exactly twelve months after the balance-sheet date?

No. Under the stipulated applicable date, the evaluation reaches March 20, Year 3. The January loan maturity is inside that period and cannot be excluded on the proposed basis.
