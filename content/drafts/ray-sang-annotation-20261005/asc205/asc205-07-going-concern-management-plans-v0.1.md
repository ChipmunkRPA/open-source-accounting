# ASC 205 presentation lesson 7: test both implementation and effect of management plans

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

When the initial going-concern evaluation raises substantial doubt, a proposed response must be tested on more than its advertised cash amount. The model described here asks whether it is probable that the plan will be effectively implemented within the one-year evaluation period and whether, once implemented, it is probable that the plan will mitigate the conditions that raised the doubt within that period. Implementation must also be early enough to address obligations when they fall due.

Those questions are distinct. A large asset sale may be sufficient if completed but lack a credible buyer. A straightforward cost reduction may be feasible yet too small or too late. A plan needs evidence for both implementation and mitigation. An optimistic spreadsheet is not evidence of a lender's commitment or a counterparty's ability to perform.

The assessment considers the plan's whole effect. Selling productive equipment can generate proceeds while reducing future receipts. Cost reductions can require severance or other initial payments. Refinancing can add interest, fees, covenants or repayment obligations. Ignoring those linked effects overstates the plan's benefit.

## Work through a proposal that is large enough only on paper

Fictional Marigold Packaging has completed its initial evaluation. Assume that evaluation, before unimplemented plans, identifies a $90,000 cash shortfall at a critical payment date and raises substantial doubt. The remaining evaluation period has also been examined; this one date is the controlling shortfall in the simplified example.

Management proposes selling a small machine for $120,000 net of direct selling costs before the payment date. However, losing that machine would reduce net operating cash receipts by $35,000 before the same date. The net benefit is therefore $85,000, leaving a $5,000 shortfall. Merely comparing the $120,000 proceeds with the $90,000 gap would miss a material dependency in the plan.

Management also proposes a cost reduction producing $45,000 of net cash savings before the payment date, after implementation costs and all relevant operating effects. If the two plans can be implemented together without overlap or further adverse interactions, their combined net benefit would be $130,000. That would exceed the identified $90,000 gap by $40,000.

The arithmetic establishes what would happen under the stipulated assumptions. It does not establish that either plan is probable. Suppose no buyer has been identified and the machine cannot realistically be removed before the payment date. That timing problem defeats reliance on the sale as currently proposed. Even if the $45,000 cost reduction is supported, it alone leaves $45,000 of the original shortfall uncovered.

Alternatively, suppose reliable evidence later supports timely completion of both plans, their stated cash effects, and the absence of new shortfalls elsewhere in the evaluation period. That new evidence can support the next judgment about whether the plans alleviate the doubt. The conclusion changes because implementation and effect are supported, rather than because the same unverified forecast has been rewritten more confidently.

## Document the evidence and the dependency

For a financing plan, examine the arrangement's status, necessary approvals, conditions, timing, available amount and the counterparty's ability to fund. For an asset sale, examine marketability, restrictions, execution time and operational consequences. For cost reductions, identify actions within management's control and the net timing of savings.

Multiple plans can share the same resource. Two forecasts must not both assume proceeds from selling the same asset. Nor should a proposed lender facility rely on collateral that another plan assumes will be sold free of restriction. The assessment needs a coherent combined case, including the effect of relevant conditions elsewhere in the forecast.

## Check your understanding

If a plan would produce $200,000 but cannot be implemented until after the critical payment date, does its size alone resolve Marigold's $90,000 shortfall?

No. Cash that arrives too late does not meet the obligation when due. A separately supported bridge or changed payment arrangement would need its own analysis.
