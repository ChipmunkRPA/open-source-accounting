# ASC 205 presentation lesson 1: connect the statements without treating them as interchangeable

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

General-purpose financial reporting explains several related questions: what the entity has and owes at a date, how it performed during a period, how its equity changed, and where cash came from and went during that period. Notes explain policies, judgments and other information needed to understand those statements. A useful presentation makes these relationships intelligible without suggesting that one statement answers every question.

A balance sheet describes a position at a date. An income statement and a statement of cash flows describe activity over a period. Equity connects successive positions through earnings and other changes. Comprehensive income can include items outside net income; the permitted form of presenting it and other statement-specific requirements require their own analysis. The simple example below deliberately stipulates that no such additional items exist.

This lesson uses general presentation as its organizing subject. It does not claim that Topic 205 alone establishes every statement, line item or format for every reporting entity. Business, nonprofit, industry and securities-reporting requirements can change the necessary package.

## Work through the statement connections

Fictional Cedar Workshop starts its first year with a $50,000 owner cash contribution. It borrows $30,000 and buys equipment for $24,000 cash. It provides services for $96,000, collects $84,000 from customers, and has $12,000 of collectible receivables at year end. It pays $60,000 of operating expenses and records $6,000 of equipment depreciation.

To isolate presentation, assume those amounts have already been recognized and classified correctly under the applicable accounting requirements. There are no taxes, interest, dividends, other assets, other liabilities, other equity changes or other comprehensive income. The exercise is intentionally simpler than a complete real reporting file.

The year-end cash balance is $80,000: the $50,000 contribution plus $30,000 borrowing plus $84,000 collections, less $24,000 equipment spending and $60,000 operating payments. Net equipment is $18,000 after depreciation. Total assets are $110,000, consisting of $80,000 cash, $12,000 receivables and $18,000 net equipment.

Net income is $30,000: $96,000 revenue less $60,000 operating expenses and $6,000 depreciation. Ending equity is therefore $80,000, the $50,000 contribution plus $30,000 income. The balance sheet balances: $110,000 assets equals $30,000 debt plus $80,000 equity.

The same activity explains cash differently. Operating cash flow is $24,000, investing cash outflow is $24,000, and financing cash inflow is $80,000. The net cash increase is $80,000. Starting from net income, the operating reconciliation adds back $6,000 depreciation and deducts the $12,000 receivable increase: $30,000 + $6,000 − $12,000 = $24,000.

## What the reader can and cannot conclude

The $80,000 cash balance is not the year's profit. It includes owner financing and borrowed money. The $30,000 profit is not the year's cash collection either. It reflects earned amounts not yet collected and depreciation that does not use current-period cash.

Nor does the balance-sheet equality establish complete reporting. An omitted transaction can leave a worksheet balanced. The preparer still needs appropriate statement captions, comparative information where applicable, accounting-policy explanations and required disclosures. Reconciliation checks are useful error detectors, not a substitute for recognition, measurement or disclosure work.

## Check your understanding

If Cedar receives another $10,000 loan on the final day, with no other change, does its profit rise by $10,000?

No. Under the stipulated facts, cash and debt each rise by $10,000. Financing cash flow changes, but borrowing does not become revenue merely because it increases cash.
