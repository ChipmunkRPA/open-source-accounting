# ASC 205 presentation lesson 3: test whether a disposal qualifies as discontinued operations

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

A business disposal does not automatically qualify for discontinued-operations reporting. In the familiar general model for an existing business, the analysis considers whether a component, or a group of components, has been disposed of or meets the applicable held-for-sale criteria, and whether the disposal represents a strategic shift with a major effect on operations and financial results. These conditions require an examination of the actual facts.

A component needs distinguishable operations and cash flows for the relevant accounting analysis. An organizational label alone does not settle that question. Similarly, management's announcement that an activity is “noncore” does not establish the strategic-shift conclusion.

The accounting for holding an asset for sale and the presentation of discontinued operations answer different questions. A qualifying held-for-sale classification may affect an asset's measurement or display without making the disposal a discontinued operation. A loss-making activity also does not qualify simply because removing its results would make continuing operations look better.

## Work through two different disposals

Fictional Ember Services runs a domestic service network and an international equipment business. Management first sells one small service location. Customers can use two nearby locations; the product offering and geographic coverage remain substantially the same. The location generated $400,000 of revenue in a business with $20 million of annual revenue, or 2%.

Those facts point away from a major strategic shift, but the 2% calculation is not an automatic exclusion rule. The team still needs to understand the component, related transactions and any wider disposal plan. A small recorded revenue share could obscure an important new product, technology or geographic activity; conversely, strategic language in a press release cannot supply missing operational significance.

Now consider a separate proposal to exit the entire international equipment business. Assume its operations and cash flows are distinguishable. The proposal would remove a different product line, all sales activity in several countries, $8 million of the entity's $20 million revenue, and a dedicated manufacturing and service organization.

The 40% revenue share, geographic exit and removal of the product line are relevant evidence supporting a potentially major strategic shift. None should be converted into a universal numerical threshold. The preparer should also assess financial results, the importance of the activity, remaining operations and the disposal's full circumstances.

Suppose negotiations have started but the conditions for the relevant disposal or held-for-sale classification are not yet satisfied. The evidence of strategic importance does not cure that missing condition. Classification must be supported for the reporting date at issue; a desired future transaction is not the same as a qualifying current disposal event.

## Build a conclusion from separate questions

Write down the operations being evaluated and why they form the relevant component or group. Then identify the disposal route and the evidence that its applicable conditions are met. Separately explain why the resulting change does or does not have the required strategic significance.

Avoid a checklist that substitutes a single revenue percentage for this reasoning. Also avoid assuming that the criteria for a sale, abandonment and distribution to owners are identical in every respect. Those routes and any special provisions need their own current authoritative analysis. This lesson intentionally does not cover special acquisition-related routes or every exception.

## Check your understanding

Ember's board approves a sale and calls it a “strategic simplification.” Is that enough to separate the activity's results as discontinued operations?

No. The label and approval provide facts to evaluate. They do not establish all required classification conditions, the appropriate reporting date or a major effect on operations and financial results.
