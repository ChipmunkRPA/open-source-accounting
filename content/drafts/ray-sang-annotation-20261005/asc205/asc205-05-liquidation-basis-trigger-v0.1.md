# ASC 205 presentation lesson 5: distinguish financial distress from imminent liquidation

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

## The specific accounting idea

Financial distress and liquidation are related but different accounting questions. Serious doubt about an entity's ability to continue operating does not, by itself, establish that liquidation is imminent. The liquidation-basis analysis asks about the likelihood and status of an actual liquidation process, within the applicable scope and exceptions.

In the framework described here, a voluntary liquidation plan involves approval by the parties with authority to make the plan effective, a remote likelihood that others will block its execution, and a remote likelihood of returning from liquidation. An imposed liquidation requires attention to its own route and the prospect of returning from that process. These are factual and legal assessments; a financial-statement preparer should not infer them from a draft board paper.

The trigger matters because liquidation-basis reporting is intended to explain the resources expected from liquidation and obligations to be settled in that process. It is not merely the ordinary reporting package with “liquidation” added to its title. The recognition, measurement, statement and disclosure consequences require a separate analysis once the basis is applicable.

## Work through three stages of one situation

Fictional Northfield Display makes retail fixtures. It has lost a major customer and expects a cash shortage. Management is negotiating financing, considering a sale of the business, and discussing a possible wind-down. At this stage, a wind-down is one alternative. The company must evaluate its going-concern situation, but the listed facts do not establish that a liquidation plan is effective and liquidation is imminent.

At a later meeting, the board approves a liquidation proposal. Assume the company's governing arrangements require shareholder approval before the proposal can become effective. That approval has not occurred, and several major shareholders oppose the plan. The board resolution alone does not resolve the missing authority or the possibility that execution will be blocked. The analysis should describe those unresolved facts rather than treating the word “approved” as the complete test.

Finally, assume shareholders with the required authority approve the plan, necessary permissions are in place, credible evidence supports a remote likelihood of the plan being blocked, and the chance of Northfield returning from liquidation is remote. Also assume a separate scope analysis finds no applicable exception. Those stipulated facts support moving to the liquidation-basis analysis at the appropriate date. The date should be tied to when the relevant conditions became satisfied, rather than automatically moved back to the date the major customer was lost.

This sequence illustrates why an entity can be financially distressed before a liquidation trigger occurs. It also shows why later events may change the accounting analysis. It does not establish the legal effectiveness of any real company's resolutions or approvals.

## Do not import a trigger from a different question

A bankruptcy filing can involve a reorganization intended to preserve operations, a liquidation or a process whose outcome is unresolved. The filing's label alone does not settle the accounting basis. Similarly, the classification of one asset as held for sale does not mean that the entire reporting entity is liquidating.

An entity formed with a contractually limited life can raise additional scope questions. Do not apply this simplified indefinite-life business example to it without checking the relevant provisions. This lesson deliberately leaves liquidation measurement and specialized scope exceptions for separate treatment.

## Check your understanding

Northfield's auditor concludes that substantial doubt about going concern remains. Does that conclusion alone require liquidation-basis financial statements?

No. The going-concern conclusion and the liquidation-basis trigger require distinct analyses. Remaining doubt is important, but the necessary liquidation facts and applicable scope still need to be established.
