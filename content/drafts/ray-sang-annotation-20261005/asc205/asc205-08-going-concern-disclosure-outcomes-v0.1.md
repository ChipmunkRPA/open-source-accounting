# ASC 205 presentation lesson 8: match the going-concern disclosure to the supported outcome

**Ray Sang Annotation**

**Original educational draft · Version 0.1.2 · October 5, 2026**

This lesson uses an original fictional example. No FASB or ASC text was retrieved or reconstructed for this lesson. Current requirements, amendments and paragraph mapping have not been verified. No human professional review is recorded. The lesson number is editorial, not an ASC paragraph number.

## The specific accounting idea

The disclosure analysis follows the supported going-concern conclusion. There is an important difference between substantial doubt that is alleviated after considering qualifying management plans and substantial doubt that remains. A preparer should preserve that distinction rather than using the same reassuring paragraph in both situations.

In the model explained here, alleviation does not automatically eliminate going-concern-related disclosure. When conditions initially raised substantial doubt and qualifying plans alleviate it, the financial statements explain the principal conditions, management's evaluation and the plans that resolved the doubt. When doubt remains, the disclosure explicitly communicates that substantial doubt remains about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern over the one-year period measured from issuance or availability for issuance, as applicable. It also explains the relevant conditions, management's evaluation and its plans. A general reference to uncertainty is not enough to convey that conclusion.

These descriptions are original teaching summaries, not prescribed note language. The exact disclosure requirements and the entity's facts still control. A note should not imply that financing is secured when it is only under discussion, or present planned savings as accomplished results.

## Work through two outcomes from the same starting point

Fictional Birch Harbor Foods has a large debt maturity within its applicable going-concern evaluation period. Expected available resources are insufficient to meet that maturity alongside operating obligations. Assume a supported initial assessment concludes that substantial doubt is raised.

In the first outcome, management is discussing an extension with the lender. The lender has issued a nonbinding expression of interest, but significant approval and funding conditions remain unresolved. Assume the evidence does not support reliance on the extension under the applicable management-plan criteria and no other adequate plan exists. Substantial doubt therefore remains in this exercise.

Birch Harbor's disclosure needs to communicate that outcome clearly. Relevant facts include the debt maturity, the resource shortfall, the status and limits of the proposed extension, and management's evaluation and plans. Replacing those facts with “management is confident in continued success” would obscure the unresolved condition. Optimistic intent is not equivalent to supported alleviation.

In the second, alternative outcome, the proposed extension has not been fully implemented at the applicable issuance or availability-for-issuance assessment date. Assume reliable evidence supports both probable implementation before the original debt maturity and probable mitigation of the conditions that raised doubt over the evaluation period. The terms, timing, required payments and other forecast effects are fully evaluated. Under those stipulated facts, the qualifying plan alleviates substantial doubt. This exercise assumes the evidence meets both criteria; neither a signature nor a nonbinding expression of interest establishes that conclusion by itself.

The disclosure should now reflect the supported outcome and explain the conditions that gave rise to the doubt and how the qualifying plan alleviated it. It should accurately describe the supported prospective extension, its implementation status and the remaining steps. It must not describe the extension as already effective. Simply deleting the entire note because the plan is expected to address the problem would lose information about the significant conditions and management's response under this model.

If an extension instead is already fully implemented by the assessment date, include its effects in the initial evaluation described in lesson 6. Do not automatically treat that different fact pattern as the same two-stage alleviation example; determine the disclosure consequences from its actual circumstances and the other applicable requirements.

## Keep the basis of accounting distinct

Substantial doubt that remains does not, by itself, change the statements to liquidation basis. The relevant liquidation conditions require their own analysis. Conversely, a comforting going-concern note cannot override a properly established requirement to use a different basis.

Management is responsible for its evaluation and financial-statement disclosures. An auditor's reporting responsibilities are related but separate. A reader should not treat an auditor's report as a substitute for the entity's required note, or assume that the absence of a particular audit paragraph establishes that no management evaluation was needed.

If the conditions continue into later reporting periods, the assessment and explanation need to reflect the later facts. Reusing an old note without checking a changed maturity, failed plan or completed refinancing can leave the financial statements telling an outdated story.

## Check your understanding

Does a supported conclusion that management's plans alleviate substantial doubt always permit Birch Harbor to omit discussion of the original liquidity problem?

No. Under the model described, disclosure still explains the significant conditions, management's evaluation and the qualifying plans that alleviated the doubt. The outcome changes the explanation; it does not automatically make the history irrelevant.
