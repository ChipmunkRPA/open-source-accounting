# Worked case: separate the liability bridge from the correction decision

**AI-assisted educational draft · v1.1.0 · 2026-10-04 · AI editorial checks only; no human professional review.**

## Stipulated facts

This original fictional example is in USD. An unsupported accrual increased expense and a liability by 12000 in each of three years. None of the accruals has reversed. For the exercise, this is an error rather than an estimate change. Tax effects, other errors and interim reporting are excluded; real work must address them. The original phrase “expense-adjusted profit” is unnecessary: each stipulated expense overstatement reduces pretax income by the same amount.

## Reconstruct the history

| Year | Opening liability overstatement | Unsupported accrual added | Closing liability overstatement | Pretax-income understatement for that year before any correction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| One | 0 | 12000 | 12000 | 12000 |
| Two | 12000 | 12000 | 24000 | 12000 |
| Three | 24000 | 12000 | 36000 | 12000 |

The year-three closing error is 36000, including 24000 from earlier years. The current-year expense effect before correction is 12000. These quantities answer different questions and should not be summed to produce a supposed 48000 error.

SEC SAB 108, Topic 1.N, Questions 1 and 2, supports evaluating both ending balance and current-period effects, including prior-year reversals. SAB 99 supplies the separate quantitative and qualitative materiality analysis. These staff views were checked on the official pages on October 4, 2026; they do not turn this exercise into an entity-specific conclusion. [sec-sab108; sec-sab99]

## Test a proposed release rather than accepting its label

Suppose someone proposes a year-three debit to the liability and credit to expense of 36000. Under the stipulated history:

- The liability overstatement becomes zero: 36000 − 36000 = 0
- The original year-three expense error becomes a 24000 understatement: 12000 − 36000 = −24000
- Pretax income becomes 24000 higher than the amount it would have been without any of these errors

The release removes the balance-sheet difference but moves the earlier-years' 24000 through current income. The calculation does not approve that journal entry. A reviewer must analyze the error history and correction consequences under current applicable accounting and filing requirements.

## A denominator packet, deliberately without a verdict

Add fictional reported pretax income of 180000, 90000 and 60000 in years one, two and three, before correction. Because each year included an extra 12000 expense, income without that year's error would be 192000, 102000 and 72000.

The 12000 annual error is approximately 6.67%, 13.33% and 20% of the respective reported amounts. The 36000 closing liability error is 60% of the year-three reported pretax-income amount. Those ratios are teaching calculations using stated denominators. They do not select a universal benchmark, equate a balance-sheet error with an income error, or establish materiality.

A trend analysis would also need the company's underlying circumstances. The data do not show contractual definitions, management incentives, segment information, other errors or relevant disclosures. Missing facts must remain missing rather than become assumptions favorable to a preferred result.

## Questions the draft should leave open

Which financial statements and periods were issued? What analysis supported earlier decisions? What does current applicable authority require for correcting this error and presenting comparatives? Would the contemplated correction itself materially misstate the current period? Does any filing or nonreliance decision require legal review? None of those questions is answered by “the closing liability now ties.”

The useful deliverable is the signed history, an explicit correction scenario, materiality questions and a source-access list. It is not a restatement verdict, filing instruction or permission to make a real posting.
