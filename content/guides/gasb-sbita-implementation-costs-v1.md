# Government software subscriptions: classify the work, not the invoice label

**Ray Sang’s Annotation · Original educational explanation · Version 1.0.0 · 2026-10-04**

AI-assisted by Open Source Accounting contributors. No professional review recorded; the brand does not mean Ray Sang personally reviewed this article.

## The accounting idea

A software-project invoice may contain several different activities. Treating its entire total as “implementation” can hide costs that belong in current expense. Under a government subscription arrangement, identify the work performed and its stage before deciding how it enters the financial statements.

Texas Comptroller's SBITA implementation-stage guidance distinguishes preliminary work, initial implementation and operational/additional implementation. It specifically treats training as expense regardless of stage and identifies a potential capitalization exception for later functionality or efficiency improvements. This is government application guidance; do not copy its policy choices into a nongovernmental software-cost model.

## Original worked example

Use the government-wide/economic-resources reporting perspective in this example, not modified-accrual governmental-fund statements. Assume a city has already concluded that a three-year arrangement is a qualifying, material SBITA. Its opening subscription liability is $180,000. There are no prepaid subscription payments or vendor incentives. Four separate invoices and time records establish:

- $9,000 evaluating competing vendors before selection
- $24,000 for necessary configuration and testing that qualify for capitalization before the system becomes usable
- $6,000 training staff before launch
- $4,000 routine support after launch

Under these stipulated classifications, the initial subscription asset is $204,000: $180,000 plus $24,000. Current expense from the other listed work totals $19,000: $9,000 + $6,000 + $4,000. The $24,000 is not added to the subscription liability simply because it was capitalized; it was separately paid implementation work.

Assume the whole asset is available for use at the start of a three-year subscription term, with no residual value, impairment or modification. Straight-line annual amortization is $68,000. This deliberately simple asset calculation excludes interest on the subscription liability, which requires its own dated payment schedule.

## A useful invoice correction

Now suppose the $24,000 configuration invoice is discovered to include $3,000 of staff training. The corrected qualifying implementation cost is $21,000. The asset becomes $201,000 and the listed current expenses become $22,000. Under the same three-year assumption, annual amortization becomes $67,000.

The invoice did not change; the facts behind its lines became clearer. This is why a spreadsheet that uses vendor invoice descriptions as automatic accounting classifications is unreliable.

## Stage is not merely a calendar month

Different modules may become usable at different times. Work performed before a launch date can still be training, while later work may add qualifying functionality. A maintenance ticket and a major expansion request should not share an accounting conclusion just because both are coded to the IT budget.

Document the approved project, modules, vendor-selection date, acceptance evidence, tasks, personnel time and paid amounts. Separate subscription payments from other project outlays. Check exclusions, renewal options, local materiality policies and current standards before using this teaching case to record an actual arrangement.
