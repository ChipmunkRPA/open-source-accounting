# Nontrust OPEB: reconcile the liability, deferred resources and benefit payments

**Ray Sang’s Annotation**

**Original educational explanation · Version 1.0.0 · 2026-10-05**

AI-assisted by Open Source Accounting contributors. Professionally unreviewed; no human review is recorded, and the label does not imply personal review by Ray Sang. This bounded historical-guidance exercise is not an official GASB standard, an actuarial valuation or a current accounting opinion.

## The benefit payment is one movement, not the whole expense

Other postemployment benefits, or OPEB, can create a long-term obligation even when cash is paid only as benefits come due. A useful close therefore needs separate schedules for the liability, cash payments, expense and amounts carried for recognition in later periods.

The New York State Comptroller's July 2018 bulletin addresses single-employer defined-benefit arrangements without a qualifying trust. Its accrual discussion includes total OPEB liability, expense and deferred inflows/outflows; it explains that measurement-period benefit payments reduce the liability. [Bulletin, printed/PDF pages 2–3, including footnote 8](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/local-government/publications/pdf/GASB-75-OPEB.pdf#page=2).

Only that bounded explanatory model is used here. The bulletin's local legal statements, implementation dates, reporting forms, administrative-expense discussion and transition entries are excluded. Continued availability of a 2018 document does not establish that every statement in it remains current. No current right to establish a trust is asserted.

## Synthetic facts and the actuarial boundary

Assume an invented single-employer, defined-benefit OPEB arrangement with no qualifying trust. Use government-wide accrual reporting. Measurement dates coincide with fiscal year-ends, so there are no payments between measurement date and reporting date. There are no nonemployer contributions, trust investments, allocation between employers, new benefit terms, administrative costs or other unlisted movements.

The actuarial amounts and expense-recognition portions below are independently supplied exercise inputs. They are not calculated by this article. In particular, the deferred-resource fractions are stipulated outputs rather than a rule to apply to every assumption change or participant population.

All amounts in the next two tables are **thousands of dollars**.

| Total liability rollforward | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Opening total OPEB liability | 2,400 |
| Service cost | 160 |
| Interest | 96 |
| Assumption-related liability increase | 120 |
| Experience-related liability decrease | (60) |
| Benefits paid during the measurement period | (180) |
| Closing total OPEB liability | 2,536 |

The liability increases by 136 even though 180 of benefits is paid. That is possible because the 376 of increases exceeds the combined 240 of experience decrease and payments. The paid benefits reduce the obligation; they are not added again to the measured liability.

## Track the unrecognized portions by origin

Of the new 120 assumption-related increase, the supplied expense schedule recognizes 30 now and carries 90 as a new deferred outflow. Of the new 60 experience-related decrease, the supplied schedule recognizes a 15 reduction of expense now and carries 45 as a new deferred inflow.

Opening deferred outflows from earlier years are 120; the supplied current recognition is 30, leaving 90. Opening deferred inflows are 80; current recognition is 20, leaving 60. No other movements occur.

| Deferred-resource rollforward | Outflows | Inflows |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening amounts | 120 | 80 |
| Current recognition of prior-year amounts | (30) | (20) |
| New amounts deferred after current recognition | 90 | 45 |
| Closing amounts | 180 | 105 |

The change in deferred outflows is +60, while deferred inflows rise 25. These changes do not represent contributions to a trust. They are accounting balances awaiting the stipulated future recognition pattern.

## Compute expense and prove it two ways

Expense in thousands is service cost 160 + interest 96 + current assumption-loss recognition 30 − current experience-gain recognition 15 + prior-outflow recognition 30 − prior-inflow recognition 20 = **281**.

A second control uses the balance movements: benefits paid 180 + increase in total liability 136 − increase in deferred outflows 60 + increase in deferred inflows 25 = **281**. The sign on deferred outflows matters. Adding 60 instead of subtracting it would overstate this reconciliation by 120.

The combined liability-minus-deferred-resources measure is 2,400 + 80 − 120 = 2,360 at opening and 2,536 + 105 − 180 = 2,461 at closing. Its 101 increase plus 180 of benefit payments explains the same 281 expense. This combined measure is an original reconciliation control, not a claim that the separate statement lines should be offset.

## Illustrative entries in dollars

One summarized entry for the stipulated noncash accrual debits OPEB expense $281,000 and deferred outflows $60,000, and credits total OPEB liability $316,000 and deferred inflows $25,000. Debits and credits each equal $341,000. The $316,000 liability credit captures all nonpayment liability movements, not the final net increase.

The separate payment entry debits total OPEB liability $180,000 and credits cash $180,000. Together, those entries raise the liability by $136,000 to $2,536,000 and reproduce both deferred-resource closing balances. Actual detailed entries should preserve each origin-year schedule; the summary is a cross-check, not a substitute for that lineage.

If the preparer instead records the entire $180,000 payment as another expense after the summarized accrual, total expense becomes $461,000 and the liability is not reduced correctly. That is a duplicated-payment error in this exercise, not a difference of estimation method.

## A payment sensitivity with unchanged cost inputs

In a parallel case, benefits paid are $210,000 rather than $180,000. Stipulate that every other actuarial and deferred-resource input remains unchanged. Closing total liability becomes $2,506,000, a $106,000 increase. Expense remains $281,000 because 210 + 106 − 60 + 25 still equals 281.

This sensitivity illustrates the identity under deliberately fixed inputs. In a real valuation, a change in benefits paid could be associated with other experience changes; those would need new evidence rather than being assumed away. Paying $30,000 more is not, by itself, proof of $30,000 more accrual expense.
