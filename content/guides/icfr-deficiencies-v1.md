## Describe what is known without filling the gaps

A fictional company's documented process calls for a monthly review of customer credits. For the exercise, the July and August reviews were not performed. An issue log was created on September 12. A redesigned procedure was approved on September 20, and its first scheduled run is October 5. The assessment date under discussion is September 30. The year and engagement type are intentionally unspecified.

A September 28 retrospective worksheet lists ten August packets and says no exception was found. It does not identify the complete July population, why those packets were selected, the review criteria, the actual records inspected or any follow-up. Treat its words as a claim made by a record. Neither the existence of a worksheet nor an approval signature supplies the missing facts.

Keep these questions separate:

- **Exposure:** What could have gone wrong while the planned activity was absent, in which accounts and disclosures, and over what population?
- **Observed outcome:** What errors were actually identified by specified procedures, with what limitations?
- **Other safeguards:** What other activities actually operated, with what precision, inputs and evidence?
- **Redesign:** What changed in the process, when was it implemented, and which identified risk does it address?
- **Subsequent operation:** What was actually performed after implementation, by whom, on which records and with what exceptions?

These headings are an original way to organize facts. They do not supply the legal or auditing definition of a deficiency category.

## Why the detected amount is only one data point

Add a fictional population: 40 July credits and 60 August credits. Assume the list is complete solely to demonstrate counting. Ten August items represent 10/60, or 16.67%, of the August list and 10/100, or 10%, of the two-month list. Neither ratio covers any July item. Neither is a dollar measure, an error-rate estimate or statistical assurance.

If one listed item has a 500 posting difference, that observation alone does not establish a 500 maximum exposure. An exposure analysis needs the population, transaction characteristics and plausible failure paths. It also needs evidence before extrapolating: this exercise provides no statistical sampling design and no basis to multiply 500 by every transaction.

## Preserve the chronology

Approval on September 20 supports an approval event. A planned October 5 run supports a future plan. A file later named “September review” might cover September transactions while documenting October work. Preserve the covered period, actual performance date, availability date and sign-off date as different fields.

Later evidence may be relevant to an earlier-date assessment; this article does not prohibit considering it. The reviewer must evaluate what it establishes about the relevant date using the applicable requirements. Do not backdate a later event, or infer an earlier completed control from a calendar invitation.

## Produce a reviewable issue packet

Write the issue, known facts, competing explanations, exposure questions and proposed evidence work. Use “requested” or “planned” for work not yet performed. Record evidence contradicting the preferred account as prominently as evidence supporting it. A revised procedure and training attendance may show steps taken, but they do not answer every question about operation.

Keep the final severity classification, remediation conclusion, management assertion, audit opinion and reporting consequences open for qualified review against authorized current sources. This article has not verified a required sample size, operating period, severity threshold or amendment effective date.
