# Loss contingencies: turn supported estimates into a liability rollforward

**Ray Sang’s Annotation**

**Original educational explanation · Version 1.0.0 · 2026-10-05**

AI-assisted by Open Source Accounting contributors. Professionally unreviewed: no human review is recorded, and this label does not mean Ray Sang personally reviewed the article. Agent use remains denied by default.

## A range needs an evidentiary meaning

A demand, a negotiating offer and an internal budget are not automatically endpoints of an accounting loss range. This exercise begins after the necessary underlying assessments have been supplied. It illustrates how an accountant can translate those assessments into a traceable register without quietly replacing them with averages.

OCC's selected response uses the better estimate within a reasonably estimated range for a probable loss; when no amount is better than another, it uses the minimum. [OCC Topic 6A, question 1](https://www.occ.treas.gov/publications-and-resources/publications/banker-education/files/pub-bank-accounting-advisory-series.pdf#page=117). The article applies that narrow measurement premise, rather than reproducing the source's legal-appeal scenario or giving a legal probability assessment.

## Define three independent fictional matters

A fictional bank tracks three unrelated contract disputes, Aster, Birch and Cove. The recognition-date analysis is stipulated to establish an incurred loss that is probable and reasonably estimable for each matter. The estimates below are supplied supported loss estimates, not claimants' demands or settlement offers. No range is a statistical probability distribution.

Each matter is assessed independently. There are no guarantees, insurance recoveries, gain contingencies, tax effects, discounting, legal fees or shared settlement caps. All liabilities start at zero, and there are no payments in the first period. The dollar amounts and chronology are invented for this explanation.

| Matter | Supported low estimate | Supported high estimate | Supplied assessment within range | First accrual |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| Aster | $260,000 | $440,000 | No point is a better estimate | $260,000 |
| Birch | $180,000 | $360,000 | $295,000 is the better estimate | $295,000 |
| Cove | $75,000 | $115,000 | No point is a better estimate | $75,000 |
| Total | $515,000 | $915,000 | Separate matter-level conclusions | $630,000 |

The arithmetic total of low endpoints is $515,000, but the required register balance is $630,000 because Birch uses its $295,000 better estimate. The $115,000 difference is Birch's better estimate less its $180,000 lower endpoint.

Debit loss-contingency expense and credit the respective liabilities for $260,000, $295,000 and $75,000. The combined entry balances at $630,000. It is noncash. No debit to cash, receivable or an expected insurance recovery is introduced.

## Diagnose the averaging error with a separate calculation

A spreadsheet that automatically takes each midpoint produces $350,000 for Aster, $270,000 for Birch and $95,000 for Cove, totaling $715,000. The errors relative to the supplied conclusions are plus $90,000, minus $25,000 and plus $20,000. Net liability and expense are overstated by $85,000.

The Birch row is especially useful: a midpoint does not always overstate the result. It can conceal an understatement behind overstatements elsewhere. A total-only review would miss that different mistakes happen to offset by $25,000.

Using the minimum for every matter is another error. It produces $515,000 and understates Birch and the total by $115,000. The correct choice depends on the supplied better-estimate assessment, not on a preference for the smallest number or the center of a range.

## Reassess the closing balance rather than adding a new full reserve

Before the next reporting date, updated supported analyses are supplied. Aster's new range is $310,000–$470,000 with no better point. Birch retains a range that includes a revised better estimate of $250,000. Cove settles fully for $82,000 in cash, with no remaining obligation.

| Matter | Opening liability | Current expense / (release) | Settlement cash | Closing liability |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Aster | $260,000 | $50,000 | $0 | $310,000 |
| Birch | $295,000 | ($45,000) | $0 | $250,000 |
| Cove | $75,000 | $7,000 | $82,000 | $0 |
| Total | $630,000 | $12,000 | $82,000 | $560,000 |

Aster's entry debits expense and credits its liability $50,000. Birch's entry debits its liability and credits expense $45,000. For Cove, debit its liability $75,000 and loss expense $7,000, then credit cash $82,000. Alternatively, accrue the additional $7,000 first and settle the resulting $82,000 balance; the final accounts are identical.

The period's $12,000 net expense is not the change in liabilities alone. Liabilities fall by $70,000 because $82,000 of cash settlement exceeds the $12,000 expense. The bridge is $630,000 plus $12,000 minus $82,000 equals $560,000.

Across both periods, cumulative expense is $642,000. It reconciles to $560,000 remaining liabilities plus $82,000 already paid. A workbook that adds the new Aster and Birch balances to their old balances instead of replacing their required closing estimates would double-count amounts already accrued.

## Change one conclusion and show the incremental effect

Consider an independent alternative at the second reporting date: Aster's supported range stays $310,000–$470,000, but new evidence identifies $390,000 as the better estimate. Its required closing liability becomes $390,000. The adjustment from its actual $260,000 opening balance is $130,000, which is $80,000 above the main case's adjustment.

Holding Birch and Cove unchanged, total second-period expense becomes $92,000 and closing liabilities become $640,000. The bridge is $630,000 plus $92,000 minus $82,000 equals $640,000. This sensitivity shows how a changed supported conclusion affects the accounts; it does not create or justify that conclusion.

## Retain unresolved questions visibly

Keep the underlying chronology, the actual authorized advisers' assessments, the estimation evidence, the reason one point is better or no point is better, changes from the prior period and the settlement record. A numerical range with no reliable basis must be returned for clarification. Do not invent counsel's opinion or derive a legal probability from an AI confidence score.

This exercise does not determine disclosures, materiality, current/noncurrent classification or the treatment of matters failing the supplied recognition conditions. In particular, an amount outside the recognized liability is not automatically irrelevant to disclosure, and the difference between the aggregate high estimate and the accrual is not itself a disclosure conclusion.
