# Operating cash flow: reconcile gross cash receipts with the indirect bridge

**Ray Sang’s Annotation**

## Two useful views of one cash population

Gross receipts and payments and an independent net-income bridge can explain the same operating cash total. Comparing them can reveal errors a bank tie-out misses.

SEC staff encourages consideration of direct-method or additional gross operating cash information and emphasizes classification controls. That staff discussion is not a new requirement to use the direct method. This original exercise illustrates the reconciliation under explicit classifications rather than claiming a complete current ASC 230 classification analysis.

## Stipulate a narrow operating cycle

Fictional Alder Supply has only ordinary customer sales, inventory purchases, employee wages, rent and depreciation in this miniature period. Customer collections, supplier payments, wages and rent payments are stipulated to be operating cash flows under a separately established applicable policy.

Exclude taxes, interest, acquisitions, foreign currency, supplier-finance arrangements, restricted cash, returns, credit losses, asset sales, noncash purchases and all investing or financing activity. There are no customer advances in the base case. All relevant balance movements arise solely from the specified income, purchase, accrual and payment activity.

The income statement contains sales of $320,000, cost of goods sold of $184,000, wage expense of $65,000, rent expense of $24,000 and depreciation of $12,000. Net income is $35,000. Rent is paid as incurred, with no prepaid rent or rent payable.

| Operating balance | Opening | Closing | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Customer receivables | $28,000 | $46,000 | +$18,000 |
| Inventory | $40,000 | $52,000 | +$12,000 |
| Inventory supplier payables | $30,000 | $39,000 | +$9,000 |
| Wages payable | $7,000 | $10,000 | +$3,000 |

These complete fictional populations are assumptions; account names alone do not establish classification or completeness.

## Derive gross cash from four rollforwards

Customer receivables reconcile as opening $28,000 plus sales $320,000 minus collections equals closing $46,000. Therefore collections are $302,000. Adding the receivable increase to sales would reverse the direction: more uncollected sales reduce current collections in this fixture.

Inventory reconciles as opening $40,000 plus purchases minus cost of goods sold $184,000 equals closing $52,000. Purchases are therefore $196,000. Supplier cash payments then equal opening payables $30,000 plus purchases $196,000 minus closing payables $39,000, or $187,000.

The inventory and payable bridges must be performed separately. Cost of goods sold is neither purchases nor necessarily cash paid. Here the $12,000 inventory increase raises purchases above cost of goods sold, while the $9,000 payable increase reduces cash paid relative to purchases.

Wage payments equal opening wages payable $7,000 plus expense $65,000 minus closing payable $10,000, or $62,000. Rent cash equals its stipulated $24,000 expense.

The direct operating cash presentation is thus:

| Cash component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Customer collections | $302,000 |
| Inventory supplier payments | $(187,000) |
| Wage payments | $(62,000) |
| Rent payments | $(24,000) |
| Net operating cash | $29,000 |

Total gross operating payments are $273,000. Collections of $302,000 minus payments of $273,000 independently produce $29,000.

## Build the indirect bridge independently

Start with $35,000 net income. Add back the $12,000 depreciation charge, which has no cash payment in this period. Subtract the $18,000 receivable increase and $12,000 inventory increase. Add the $9,000 supplier-payable increase and $3,000 wage-payable increase.

$35,000 + $12,000 − $18,000 − $12,000 + $9,000 + $3,000 = $29,000.

Each adjustment has a reason tied to the original population. Depreciation is a noncash expense; the working-capital adjustments reconcile timing differences. They are not additional bank receipts or payments to append to the direct presentation.

With opening cash of $80,000 and no other activity, closing cash is $109,000. That cash tie is necessary but still does not independently establish classification.

## Two changes test different weaknesses

First, imagine $9,000 of ordinary supplier payments is wrongly labeled investing. Reported operating cash would become $38,000, and investing cash would become negative $9,000. Total cash movement would remain $29,000. The bank tie therefore survives the classification error. Its materiality still requires the relevant facts and circumstances; unchanged total cash is not a complete conclusion.

Second, add a separate $10,000 customer advance, expressly stipulated to be an operating receipt and an unearned contract liability. Sales and net income remain unchanged. Direct operating receipts rise to $312,000 and operating cash to $39,000. The indirect bridge gains a $10,000 contract-liability increase and also reaches $39,000; closing cash becomes $119,000. Recognition of that advance as revenue is not assumed.
