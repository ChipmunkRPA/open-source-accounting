# Selected items and audit samples: 60% coverage is not a population conclusion

**Ray Sang’s Annotation · Original educational explanation · Version 1.0.0 · 2026-10-04**

AI-assisted by Open Source Accounting contributors. No professional review recorded; the brand does not mean Ray Sang personally reviewed this article.

## The audit idea

Testing a large percentage of recorded dollars can be useful, but coverage alone does not establish what is true about the remainder. Deliberately selecting the largest items and taking a sample intended to represent a population are different designs. Their conclusions must match their purposes.

GAO/CIGIE's Financial Audit Manual section 480 distinguishes selected-item work from audit sampling, including statistical and nonstatistical approaches. That federal methodology supplies context for research in AU-C 530. This article does not turn its example into a prescribed sampling method.

## Original worked example

A fictional purchases population contains 1,000 transactions totaling $2,000,000. Its 20 largest items total $1,200,000. The auditor tests all 20 and finds two overstatements totaling $8,000.

The selected items represent 60% of total dollars but only 2% of transaction count. Known identified error is $8,000 in the tested items. The remaining 980 transactions total $800,000. Nothing about a 60% dollar-coverage figure automatically proves that this remainder is accurate.

One tempting calculation is $8,000 ÷ $1,200,000 = approximately 0.6667%, followed by multiplication by $800,000 to claim $5,333.33 of additional error. That is a numerical extrapolation, not a defensible statistical projection from the described selection. The 20 items were chosen for size, not as a representative probability sample of the remainder.

Another tempting claim is that two exceptions out of twenty means 10% of all invoices are wrong. That conclusion also exceeds the design. The large items may have different processing, controls, vendors and error patterns from small recurring transactions.

## Design the remaining work deliberately

The auditor could perform suitable procedures on the remaining population, potentially including an appropriately designed sample. First reconcile the population, define the sampling unit, identify the assertion and consider relevant strata. The risk, tolerable misstatement, expected error and other evidence affect the design; this article supplies no automatic sample size.

Distinguish nonstatistical sampling, which still aims at a population conclusion using professional judgment, from a targeted selection intended to conclude only on selected items. Calling a selection “nonstatistical” does not remove the need to justify the work on untested balances.

## A useful reporting sentence

An internal workpaper could accurately say: “We examined the 20 largest recorded purchases, representing $1.2 million of the $2 million population, and identified $8,000 of overstatement in those selected items. Procedures on the remaining $800,000 are described separately.”

That sentence reports what happened without implying a conclusion on the untested remainder. It still needs the underlying procedure details, error causes and follow-up. The example is not an audit report, an agreed-upon-procedures report or an assurance opinion.
